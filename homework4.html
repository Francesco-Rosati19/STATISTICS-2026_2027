Ex 1: Welford's Recurrence Formulas

1. Knuth's Approach for the Mean

By definition, the mean at step k is x̄ₖ = (1/k) ∑ xᵢ. Multiplying by k and splitting the sum to isolate the last element yields the recurrence:

k · x̄ₖ = (k - 1) · x̄ₖ₋₁ + xₖ
x̄ₖ = x̄ₖ₋₁ + (xₖ - x̄ₖ₋₁) / k

2. Deriving Welford's Sum of Squares & Variance

Following the same logic, let Mₖ = Σ (xᵢ - x̄ₖ)² be the Sum of Squares (SS). We isolate the k-th term and substitute the mean difference (xᵢ - x̄ₖ) = (xᵢ - x̄ₖ₋₁) - (xₖ - x̄ₖ₋₁) / k:

Mₖ = (xₖ - x̄ₖ)² + Σ (xᵢ - x̄ₖ)² (from i=1 to k-1)
...expanding the binomial inside the sum and canceling cross-terms yields...
Mₖ = Mₖ₋₁ + (xₖ - x̄ₖ₋₁) · (xₖ - x̄ₖ)

From this exact recurrence for the Sum of Squares, the Sample Variance is trivially derived at any step k ≥ 2 by applying Bessel's correction (dividing by k - 1):

sₖ² = Mₖ / (k - 1)

3. JavaScript Implementation (3-Line Core Logic)

Translating the above recurrence formulas into code requires exactly 3 core lines. This approach prevents catastrophic floating-point cancellation. 

// Online update for a new score 'x' at step 'k' (k ≥ 1):
const delta = x - mean;
mean += delta / k;           // 1. Knuth's Mean
M2 += delta * (x - mean);    // 2. Welford's Sum of Squares

// Note: Sample Variance is calculated as s2 = M2 / (k - 1) for k ≥ 2

Statistical Conclusions

1. What conclusions can you draw from comparing the averages?

By comparing the Grand Average of Sampling Means with the theoretical μ, and the Grand Average of Sampling Variances with the theoretical σ², we observe that they match almost perfectly. This proves mathematically that both estimators are Unbiased Estimators (their expected value equals the true population parameter). Specifically for the variance, this validates the necessity of Bessel's correction (k - 1) used in Welford's algorithm.

2. Why are these results useful in inferential statistics?

In real-world inferential statistics, we never know the true population parameters, and we only have access to a single sample (e.g., a single hacker's trajectory). Knowing that our estimators are unbiased guarantees that our calculations do not systematically overestimate or underestimate the system's defensive strength. We can confidently use sample statistics to draw generalized conclusions about the whole population.

3. How does increasing systems (n) affect estimator variability?

If you repeat the experiment increasing the number of systems n (e.g., from 50 to 500), you will see the trajectories on the canvas violently compress into a flat line. Mathematically, the variability collapses as O(1/n):

• Var(x̄) scales exactly as σ² / n.
• Var(s²) also decays proportionally to 1 / n.

Conclusion: Increasing the sample size n guarantees consistency. The larger the sample, the smaller the Standard Error, meaning the sample estimates become virtually identical to the true theoretical parameters.
